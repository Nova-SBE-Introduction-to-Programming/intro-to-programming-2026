\tanote{Ninety seconds, left to right. The dates are on the slide; the names and the story are
here. 1958, Rosenblatt's perceptron: a machine that adjusts its own weights from examples instead
of being programmed rule by rule. That is still the whole idea. 1969, Minsky and Papert's book
\emph{Perceptrons}: one layer can only separate what a straight line separates, so it cannot learn
XOR. Funding collapsed; the first AI winter. Careful with the attribution: they wrote the critique
that made multi-layer networks the open problem; they did not invent them. 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton
and Williams in Nature: backpropagation sends the error back through every layer, so many layers
finally become trainable. The answer to 1969, seventeen years late. 1997, Hochreiter and
Schmidhuber: LSTM carries memory across a sequence. 2017, Vaswani et al.: the Transformer drops
recurrence for attention and trains in parallel. Then the caption, which is the payoff: none of
this is new. What arrived was compute — CUDA in 2007 let ordinary programmers run their maths on a
graphics card, and in 2012 AlexNet trained on two of them and cut the ImageNet error by nearly half (15\% against 26\%) — and
data: Common Crawl, a non-profit giving the public web away as text. Neither is an idea about
intelligence. If someone asks why this happened now and not in 1990, that is the answer. If it
comes up: Hinton shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with Hopfield for this line of work.}

\begin{frame}{Guess the next word.}
\framesubtitle{How we got here · GPT-2 · 1 of 4 · how it was trained}
\vspace*{\fill}
\nextwordfig
\vskip2.5mm
\muted{That is all training is: hide the next word, guess, get corrected. No grammar lesson,
no facts typed in — just 1.5 billion numbers, nudged a few billion times.}
\vskip1mm
{\tiny\color{NovaMuted}Radford et al.\ (2019): WebText, 8 million pages, 40 GB · Brown et al.\ (2020): GPT-3 on
Common Crawl, filtered · percentages illustrative}
\vfill
\end{frame}
\tanote{A minute. Walk the picture left to right, then down. A page from the web with the next
word covered. The model guesses: a whole list, each with a probability. The page is uncovered:
horn. If the model put too little weight on horn, every one of its numbers is nudged a hair in
the direction that would have made horn more likely. Then the next word, on the next page, for
weeks. Nobody typed in a fact or a grammar rule; everything it knows fell out of that game. The
data: OpenAI did not use Common Crawl for GPT-2. They said it was too messy and built WebText:
every outbound link from a Reddit post with at least three upvotes, 45 million links, 8 million
pages after cleaning, 40 GB of text. Common Crawl, the non-profit copy of the public web from
slide 12, is what GPT-3 was trained on a year later, after filtering. The numbers on the bars are
made up to look right; the real list has 50,257 entries, one per token, and a token is often a
piece of a word rather than a whole one.}

\hue{NovaPurple}
\begin{frame}{Words become places.}
\framesubtitle{How we got here · GPT-2 · 2 of 4 · what it learned}
\vspace*{\fill}
\meaningmap
\vskip2mm
\muted{Encode: a word becomes a position. Decode: a position becomes the nearest word. Neighbours
mean similar things; directions mean relations.}
\vskip1mm
{\tiny\color{NovaMuted}Positions illustrative · arrow arithmetic: Mikolov et al.\ (2013), king $-$ man $+$ woman $\approx$ queen}
\vfill
\end{frame}
\tanote{A minute and a half; this is the slide that makes the unicorn make sense. Every word the
model knows is a point, and a point is just a list of 1,600 numbers. The guessing game moved the
points. Words that show up in the same places on the web ended up near each other, because the
model was rewarded for treating them alike: horse, pony, donkey in one cluster, scientist and
biologist in another. Two things to point at. First, the dashed arrow: start at horse, move in the
direction that horn lives, and you arrive near unicorn. The model never memorised a definition of
unicorn; it has a position for it, and the position is roughly horse plus horn. Second, the teal
arrows: Lisbon to Portugal and Madrid to Spain are the same arrow. Relations are directions. That
is the famous king minus man plus woman is queen, and it is why the story could name a scientist,
give him a university in La Paz and put him in the Andes: each of those is the nearest word to a
position the story had moved to. Now the honest half: encode and decode by nearness is also how
you get four-horned unicorns. Numbers of horns live near horned, and nothing in the map knows that
one is the right number. Say the caveat once: a flat sketch of 1,600 dimensions, and the
positions here are drawn by hand to make the point.}

Then one block, the same block, 48 times in a row: each word looks back at the earlier words and
decides which ones matter (the arcs: the last word attends strongly to "distinctive", less to
"their"), then a small network thinks over what it collected. That look-back is attention, the
thing the 2017 Transformer from slide 12 introduced, and it is the whole architecture: nothing
else in there. At the top, a probability for every word in the vocabulary. GPT-2's 1.5 billion
numbers are the weights inside those 48 blocks plus the word positions, and every one of them was
set by the guessing game. Do not go deeper than this; nobody in the room needs the maths, they
need the shape: numbers in, look back, guess.}

\end{frame}

data files from the seed (tests stay green while the app crashes on load) and it never adds the
form field (nothing tests that screen). Both are the "green is not done" lesson. Show them, do not
fix them silently. If it edits a test: stop, discard, point at the rule, re-prompt.}
