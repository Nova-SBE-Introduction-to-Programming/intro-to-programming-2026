\tanote{Ninety seconds, left to right. The dates are on the slide; the names and the story are
here. 1958, Rosenblatt's perceptron: a machine that adjusts its own weights from examples instead
of being programmed rule by rule. That is still the whole idea. 1969, Minsky and Papert's book
\emph{Perceptrons}: one layer can only separate what a straight line separates, so it cannot learn
XOR. Funding collapsed; the first AI winter. Careful with the attribution: they wrote the critique
that made multi-layer networks the open problem; they did not invent them. 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton
and Williams in Nature: backpropagation sends the error back through every layer, so many layers
finally become trainable. The answer to 1969, seventeen years late. 1997, Hochreiter and
Schmidhuber: LSTM carries memory across a sequence. 2017, Vaswani et al.: the Transformer drops
recurrence for attention and trains in parallel. Then the caption, which is the payoff: none of
this is new. What arrived was compute — CUDA in 2007 let ordinary programmers run their maths on a
graphics card, and in 2012 AlexNet trained on two of them and cut the ImageNet error by nearly half (15\% against 26\%) — and
data: Common Crawl, a non-profit giving the public web away as text. Neither is an idea about
intelligence. If someone asks why this happened now and not in 1990, that is the answer. If it
comes up: Hinton shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with Hopfield for this line of work.}

\framesubtitle{How we got here · GPT-2 · how it was trained}
\vspace*{\fill}
\nextwordfig
\vskip3mm
\muted{That is all training is: hide the next word, guess, get corrected. Writing is the same
game run forwards, one word at a time — and nothing checks it.}
\vskip1mm
{\tiny\color{NovaMuted}Radford et al.\ (2019), \emph{Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners} · percentages illustrative}
\vfill

\tanote{A minute and a half; it is the only GPT-2 slide, so land both halves. Walk the strip
left to right, then round the loop. One: a page from the web, next word covered. Two: the model
guesses, a whole list, each with a probability. Three: the page is uncovered, horn; if the model
put too little weight on horn, every one of its 1.5 billion numbers is nudged a hair towards it.
Then the next word, the next page, for weeks. Nobody typed in a fact or a grammar rule. If asked
about the data: not Common Crawl, which OpenAI called too messy, but WebText, 8 million pages that
Reddit users had linked to, 40 GB; GPT-3 read a filtered Common Crawl a year later. The
percentages are made up; the real list has 50,257 entries, one per token. Second half, the
hand-off: writing is the same loop with no page to check against. Pick a word from the guess,
stick it on the end, run again; every word of the unicorn story came out that way. No plan, so a
fluent paragraph can walk into four horns and keep going, and nothing in the loop checks
anything. That gap is what the rest of the class fills: tools that let it look, a judge that says
red or green, and rules. The agent loop a few slides on is this loop one level up, with a tool
call instead of a word.}
